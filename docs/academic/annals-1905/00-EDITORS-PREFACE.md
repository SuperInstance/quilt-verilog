# 00 — THE EDITOR'S PREFACE

**Annals of the Kaldfjord Circle, vol. I (1903–1905)**

These annals came to light in March of this year, in the loft of the Strandsild
cannery at Kaldfjord, Finnmark, during the survey that preceded the building's
demolition. They were in a steel trunk of the kind the telegraph service used
for instruments: five fair-copied memoirs in a schoolmistress's hand, a
minute-book of Thursday meetings, seven packets of letters sorted by writer, and
a comptometer ribbon spooled on a wooden dowel. The trunk's seal was intact. The
cannery's last manager, it appears, simply closed the loft in 1908 and let the
building hold the school's papers for it.

We publish as found. The memoirs are printed in the order the minute-book gives
them, not the order of their dating: the tally-box (read February 1904), the
balance of the books (May 1904), the parallax memoirs (October 1904), the
bell-rope (February 1905; refused at Kristiania in June of that year), and the
ledger papers (July–August 1905). The correspondence is printed last, arranged
chronologically; it contains the Circle's priority dispute, one erratum of
their own finding, one conjecture proposed by a member and refuted by another,
and one rejection by the Academy that the herring fishery itself afterwards
overturned. We have resisted the temptation to smooth any of this. The Circle
printed their misadds in the margin; so do we.

The transcription policy is minimal and is stated once, here. Their notation is
kept where it is unambiguous (they write S for a sum of entries, ρ for a rate of
drift, F for a staleness in days, the signs + and − for credit and debit; they
count money in kroner and øre, fish by the barrel, distance in fathoms, time in
days and four-hour watches). Where the school used a word of their own coinage —
*box* for their worker, *gauge* for their measure of resemblance, *fold* for a
commutative summary, *cut* for a set of accounts — the word is kept and defined
at first use, in their definition, not ours. Square brackets mark the editor's
additions; everything else is theirs, including the errors.

One thing the reader should be told before going further, because the Circle
would have insisted on it: they kept no calculating machine larger than
Johanne Fosse's comptometer, and they trusted no figure that had not been
carried through three times by two different hands. Every table below was so
verified; where the passes disagreed, the disagreement is printed. Halvard
Grønn's rule, from the minute-book of 14 January 1904, stands as the school's
epigraph and might as well stand as ours:

> A rule that cannot be broken by a counterexample is no rule. A proof that
> hides its corrections is a false proof.

What follows is village mathematics in the strict sense: done at night, by
people who worked all day, on the only subject they all shared — the keeping of
honest accounts in a world that moves. The Circle believed their subject was
general. The reader will decide whether they were right; we note only that the
trunk was sealed, the books balanced, and the bell, by every account, was
rung on the hour.

— The Editors
